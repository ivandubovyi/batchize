Every question on the YC application, and what each one is really asking

The 26 questions, grouped the way the form groups them. Each one links to what a strong answer contains and the mistakes that get caught in it.

Nothing here is a template. Templates are the reason partners can spot an application written by somebody who read a blog post, and the answers that work are specific in ways no template can be. What is here is what each question is for.

Founders

Who you are, how you met, and proof you get things done.

Founder video: what will you say in your 1-minute intro? (paste your script or bullet points)Partners watch this before reading anything else. Structure: who you are, one plain sentence on what you make, your sharpest number or insight. No slides, no production value, just clarity and energy. How long have the founders known one another and how did you meet? Have any of the founders not met in person?This is a breakup-risk question. Long history working together beats long friendship. If you met recently or online, show evidence you've already shipped something together. Who writes code, or does other technical work on your product? Was any of it done by a non-founder?Partners want the product built by founders. Outsourced code is a red flag. If a non-founder built parts, be honest and explain how that changes. Please tell us about a time you most successfully hacked some (non-computer) system to your advantage.This is really asking: are you resourceful and a little relentless? Pick a story with a concrete outcome, not a personality claim. Small and real beats grand and vague.
Company

The one-liner and what you're actually building.

Company nameShort, spellable, sayable. Don't burn days here. Describe what your company does in 50 characters or less.The most-read sentence in your application. Formula that works: "[Product] is [what] for [who]." A partner should be able to repeat it after one read. Company URL, if anyA live URL, even rough, is progress evidence. If it's not live, leave it blank rather than linking a dead page. What is your company going to make? Please describe your product and what it does or will do.Describe what a user literally sees and does, in plain words. No adjectives, no vision statements. If a smart friend couldn't sketch your product after reading this, rewrite it. Where do you live now, and where would the company be based after YC?Just answer it. If you'd relocate, say so plainly.
Progress

The section partners weight most. Numbers win.

How far along are you?The answer partners weight most. Lead with your single strongest number. Launched or not, say exactly what exists today. Small honest numbers beat impressive adjectives. What tech stack are you using, or planning to use, to build this product?A plain list is fine. Partners scan for whether the team can actually build this. Are people using your product? How many active users or customers do you have?Give the real number and define it (weekly actives, paying teams). A precisely-defined small number reads better than a vague big one. Do you have revenue? How much?State it plainly with the period (MRR, total). If zero, say zero and what would change that. Never dress it up. How long have each of you been working on this? How much of that has been full-time?This is a velocity question: partners divide your progress by this answer. A lot built in a short time is the best possible signal.
Idea

Why this, why you, why now, and who else is trying.

Why did you pick this idea to work on? Do you have domain expertise in this area? How do you know people need what you're making?Founder-market fit lives here. The strongest answers start with lived experience: "When I was X, I saw Y." Cite real conversations with users, not market-size logic. What's new about what you're making? What substitutes do people resort to because it doesn't exist yet (or they don't know about it)?Naming the substitute (a spreadsheet, a manual process, a worse tool) proves the need is real. If there is no substitute, partners wonder if there is no need. Who are your competitors? What do you understand about your business that they don't?Never say "no competitors": it reads as "hasn't looked" or "no market." Name the closest ones, then give the one insight you have that they don't act on. How do or will you make money? How much could you make?A simple, concrete mechanism beats a complicated one. Show the napkin math for how this gets big: price times plausible customer count. Which category best applies to your company?One or two words (B2B SaaS, fintech, devtools, consumer, healthcare...).
Equity

Ownership, investment, and fundraising status. State it plainly.

Have you formed ANY legal entity yet? If so, describe it.Just the facts: entity type, where, who owns it. Messy structures are fixable; hiding them is not. Describe the breakdown of the equity ownership among the founders, employees and any other stockholders.State the split plainly and why. Heavily unequal splits get probed in interviews; defensive or evasive wording here reads worse than the split itself. Have you taken any investment yet? From whom, how much, and on what terms?List amounts, instruments (SAFE, priced), and caps. Partners check this against your equity answer for consistency. Are you currently fundraising?Yes or no, plus the round if yes. No spin needed.
Curious

Other ideas, other accelerators, and how you found YC.

If you had any other ideas you considered applying with, please list them.A place to show idea quality and taste. One or two crisp alternatives is plenty. Have you applied to or participated in any other startup accelerators or incubators?Be straightforwardly honest; this is verifiable. How did you hear about Y Combinator?One sentence. Nobody was ever rejected for this answer.

Buzzwords

Words like revolutionary, seamless, disrupt and leverage are deleted on sight, because they are what people write when they have not decided what the thing is.

Hedges

We think, hopefully, trying to. Hedged writing usually marks an argument between cofounders that has not been settled.

Weasel numbers

A lot of, several, many. If you know the number, use it. If you do not, that is the finding.

Unsupported superlatives

The best, the only, the first platform to. Claims that invite a partner to spend thirty seconds disproving them.

Percentages with no base

Three hundred percent growth is a fine answer if you say growth from what.

Signups presented as usage

Signups are the easiest number to grow and the least predictive. Active, paying and returning are different words for a reason.

Contradictions across answers

A user count in one answer that disagrees with another, revenue where you said you have no users, a launch date that does not match how long you say you have been building.
